# The 70-Hour Trap — Why More Hours Might Be Making You Worse at Your Job

Narayana Murthy says we should work 70 hours a week. Parkinson's Law says work expands to fill whatever time you give it. Both can't be right. Or can they?

I've been thinking about this tension for a while — not as an abstract debate, but as someone who has lived on both sides. There have been weeks where I've pushed 60+ hours and felt like I was building something meaningful. And there have been weeks where I worked 35 focused hours and shipped more than the previous month combined. The difference wasn't effort. It was something else entirely.

## The Experiment I Accidentally Ran on Myself

A few months ago, I had a week where I was genuinely swamped. Multiple deadlines, a production issue, and a presentation to prepare. I worked long hours — not because I believed in the 70-hour philosophy, but because I had no choice.

By Thursday, I noticed something strange. I was spending 45 minutes on emails that should have taken 10. I was re-reading the same documentation three times without absorbing it. I was "working" for 12 hours but producing maybe 4 hours of actual output. The rest was friction — context switching, decision fatigue, and the slow erosion of focus that comes from never stepping away.

The following week, I tried something different. I set hard boundaries: 8 hours of focused work, no exceptions. I used timers. I batched similar tasks. I said no to two meetings that didn't need me.

I shipped more in that 40-hour week than in the previous 65-hour one. And I wasn't exhausted on Saturday.

That's when Parkinson's Law stopped being a concept and became something I could feel. Work really does expand to fill the time you give it. Give yourself 12 hours, and your brain will find ways to make a 3-hour task take 12. Not through laziness — through diffusion. The task becomes less urgent, so you approach it less sharply.

## What Murthy Gets Right (And What He Misses)

I don't think Narayana Murthy is wrong about everything. His argument isn't really about hours — it's about dedication. About caring enough to go deep. About not treating your career as a 9-to-5 obligation. That part resonates.

The problem is the metric. Hours are a terrible proxy for dedication. I've seen engineers work 50 hours and produce nothing because they were stuck in meetings and Slack threads. I've seen others work 30 hours and build systems that lasted years because every hour was spent in deep, uninterrupted focus.

When Murthy built Infosys, the work was different. Software development in the 1980s and 90s was more linear — more hours often did mean more output. But knowledge work in 2024 doesn't scale linearly with time. After a certain point, more hours produce negative returns. You make mistakes. You write code you'll have to rewrite. You make decisions you'll have to reverse.

## The Real Enemy Isn't Laziness — It's Diffusion

Here's what I've come to believe: most people don't need more hours. They need fewer, sharper ones.

The Pomodoro Technique works not because 25 minutes is a magic number, but because it creates artificial scarcity. You have 25 minutes. That's it. Your brain responds to the constraint by focusing. Remove the constraint — "I have all day" — and focus evaporates.

I started applying this beyond individual tasks. Instead of planning my week as "I'll work on Project X this week," I started saying "I'll spend exactly 6 hours on Project X, spread across Tuesday and Thursday mornings." The constraint forced prioritization. What's the most important thing I can do in 6 hours? Not the most thorough. The most important.

The results surprised me. Not just in output, but in quality. When you know you only have 6 hours, you don't gold-plate. You don't over-engineer. You build what matters and move on.

## The Uncomfortable Truth About Long Hours

There's another dimension to this that nobody talks about. Long hours feel productive. There's a psychological comfort in being busy — in telling yourself and others that you worked until 10 PM. It signals dedication. It signals importance.

But feeling productive and being productive are different things. I've caught myself staying late not because I had more to do, but because leaving at 6 felt like I wasn't trying hard enough. That's not dedication. That's performance.

The hardest shift wasn't working fewer hours. It was being okay with working fewer hours. Trusting that focused, bounded work could produce better results than exhausted, unbounded work. It felt counterintuitive. It felt lazy. It wasn't.

## Where I've Landed

I don't work 70 hours a week. I don't work 40 either, most weeks. I work until the important things are done, and then I stop. Some weeks that's 35 hours. Some weeks it's 50. But the hours are a consequence of the work, not a target.

Parkinson's Law taught me that time is a container, and work is a gas — it fills whatever space you give it. Murthy taught me that caring deeply about your craft matters. Both are true. The synthesis is: care deeply, but constrain ruthlessly.

If you're working 70 hours and shipping great work, I'm not going to tell you to stop. But if you're working 70 hours and feeling like you're running in place — the problem might not be that you need more hours. It might be that you need fewer, better ones.

What would happen if you gave yourself half the time?
